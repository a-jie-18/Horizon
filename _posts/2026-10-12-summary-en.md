### [Post-Run Stretching and Recovery for Tired Runners](https://sspai.com/post/115568) ⭐️ 4.0/10

rss · 少数派 \(生活方式与效率\) · Oct 11, 07:00

**「Background」** Many runners skip post-run stretching, and those who do stretch often pick the wrong muscles, hold too briefly, or ignore fascia. The author, writing for a general fitness audience, argues that recovery is not an optional cool-down but the part of training where progress actually happens.

**「Solution」** Drawing on park observation, the author identifies three common mistakes: wrong stretches, insufficient duration, and neglected fascia. He says the muscles that actually tighten after running are the quadriceps, glutes, calves, and the deep iliopsoas in the groin, since the quads absorb landing impact through eccentric contraction. He describes specific cues for each: pulling the ankle toward the glutes with thighs drawn together for the quads, a seated figure-four fold for the glutes, a wall or step stretch for the calves, and a lunge with breath directed toward the pelvis for the iliopsoas, warning that poor form and shallow breathing blunt the effect. He recommends holding each stretch at least 30 seconds, using a phone timer or six deep breaths, and releasing fascia first with a foam roller, spending about 30 seconds on painful spots before rolling on. For runners too tired to stretch, he suggests folding recovery into daily life, using an elbow as a foam roller on the quads or pressing one knee into the calf, and moving stretching to bedtime. He also covers cool-downs to clear lactate, carbohydrate replenishment \(half a bowl of rice after a 20 km run\), vitamins and calcium, and sleep timing, suggesting runners avoid late runs or finish four hours before bed and add 30 minutes of sleep per extra hour of exercise. The piece ends with a promotional plug for the author&\#x27;s sedentary-health guide.

**「Takeaway」** The author&\#x27;s core thesis is that post-run stretching and recovery deserve the same attention as the run itself, because adaptation happens during recovery rather than during the workout. The guidance is practical and familiar rather than evidence-based, so readers should treat specific claims about lactate, glycogen, and fascia as the author&\#x27;s assertions rather than established findings.
